# Charles Baudelaire

"The Beautiful is always strange."

He wrote this in 1855, two years before the publication of *Les Fleurs du Mal*, and it is the most compressed statement of his entire philosophical project. Not that beauty can be strange, or sometimes contains strangeness as a feature. Always. The strangeness is constitutive of it. Beauty without an element of the unexpected, the unsettling, the slightly wrong is not beauty. It is prettiness, which is a completely different and inferior thing.

This distinction matters because the Romantic tradition Baudelaire inherited had spent fifty years producing prettiness and calling it beauty. Nature, the divine, the pastoral, the sublime in its approved and domesticated forms: these were the territories of acceptable beauty, and the artist's job was to receive it, to be moved by it, to render it faithfully. The artist was a conduit. The genius was a receiver. Inspiration arrived from outside and the artist's virtue was to stay out of its way.

Baudelaire looked at this and concluded it was wrong in its foundations.

The artist is not a conduit. The artist is a constructor. The conditions for visionary experience are built, not waited for. The material is not nature in its scenic forms but the city in its shadows and contradictions, its crowds and its solitude, its boredom pushed far enough that it cracks open into something that briefly resembles the infinite. The poem is an operation performed on consciousness, and the operator is the poet, not the muse.

This is the inversion that makes Baudelaire the first modern poet in any serious sense. Before him, the Romantic tradition pointed outward: toward landscapes, toward the divine, toward the received tradition of what was worth attending to. After him, the tradition turned inward, toward the constructed experience, toward the city, toward the interior life as the primary territory of the serious artist. Every significant development in Western poetry for the next hundred and fifty years passes through this inversion.

He was born in Paris in 1821. His father died when he was six. His mother remarried quickly, a military man who would eventually become a general and ambassador, and the family moved with the regularity of a posting. Baudelaire hated his stepfather without reservation and the feeling appears to have been mutual. He was expelled from his lycee for refusing to hand over a note passed to him by a classmate. He kept the note. He was that kind of person.

At twenty-one he inherited a modest fortune from his father's estate. He moved to the Ile Saint-Louis, furnished his apartment with extreme care, began wearing the black suit that became his signature, started spending. He was deliberate about the spending the way he was deliberate about everything. The dandy, as Baudelaire theorised the figure, is not a hedonist. The dandy is an ascetic of a particular kind: someone who submits every aspect of their appearance and environment to an exacting aesthetic discipline, not for pleasure but as a philosophical stance. The dandy refuses to be passive. The dandy composes the self the way a poet composes a poem.

By the time his family became alarmed enough to put his inheritance under legal management, he had spent half of it. He never escaped debt for the rest of his life.

He also began, around this time, a relationship with Jeanne Duval, a Haitian-French actress and dancer who remained central to his life for twenty years. She is the "Black Venus" of the erotic cycle in *Les Fleurs du Mal*, and the poems addressed to her are among the most complicated love poems in the tradition: ecstatic and bitter, worshipful and hostile, shot through with a recognition that desire and its object exist in permanent contradiction. Baudelaire did not resolve this. He wrote it exactly as it was. The erotic, in his system, is one of the places where the correspondence between the visible and the invisible is most violently active. The body points toward something the body cannot contain. The poem's job is to hold both without collapsing into one.

He read Swedenborg in these years and found in the Swedish mystic's theory of correspondences the theological grounding for what he had already arrived at by other routes. Swedenborg had documented his visions of the angelic world with the meticulous precision of a scientist and concluded that the natural world was, in its entirety, a system of symbols pointing toward the spiritual world. Every visible thing was a code. The world was not a fact. It was a message.

Baudelaire turned this into the poem "Correspondances." Perfumes, colours, sounds answer to each other, he wrote. The senses are not separate channels. They are simultaneous translations of the same underlying signal. This is not a metaphor for the richness of sensory experience. It is a claim about the structure of reality, and it is the claim on which the entire Symbolist movement rests. Mallarme inherited it. Verlaine inherited it. Rimbaud took it and ran with it so far that he disappeared over the horizon at twenty and was never seen as a poet again.

Spleen is the condition Baudelaire diagnosed before he found the correspondences to treat it. It is not depression in the clinical sense, not melancholy in the Romantic sense. Spleen is the specific suffering of a consciousness that has glimpsed the infinite and been returned to the finite, that has felt the valve open and then felt it close again, that knows the corridor is not the building but cannot find the door. The city produces it in abundance. The crowd, pressing from all sides, offers the simultaneous sensation of total immersion in humanity and total solitude within it. Baudelaire named this condition with precision and then spent his career mapping the territory it opened. The prose poems of *Le Spleen de Paris*, published after his death, are the deepest cartography: the city as interior landscape, the stranger's face as a philosophical problem, beauty ambushing the observer from precisely the angles the observer was not watching.

The idea that ordinary consciousness is inadequate to the deeper reality runs through everything he wrote, including *Artificial Paradises*, his extended investigation of hashish and opium. He used both deliberately and documented the results with the same precision a Naturalist would bring to documenting a social environment. The intoxicant is not a pleasure. It is a valve. It opens something that the ordinary perceptual apparatus keeps closed. The problem, which Baudelaire was honest enough to record, is that it opens the valve by damaging the mechanism. What comes through is real. The cost of the opening is also real. He did not resolve this either.

*Les Fleurs du Mal* was published in June 1857. Six weeks later, Baudelaire and his publisher were prosecuted for offending public morality. Six poems were suppressed. The government's lawyers argued that the book glorified evil. This was a misreading, but a misreading that illuminates. The book does not glorify evil. It proposes that evil, suffering, transgression, and damage are legitimate territories for philosophical investigation, that turning away from them means turning away from part of the message the world is sending, that the comfortable life conducted within the boundaries of approved experience is a life of chosen blindness.

The trial made the book famous. It has not been out of print since.

He spent the last decade of his life in steadily worsening health, syphilis compounding the effects of years of poverty and excess, and moved to Brussels hoping to lecture and earn money. Brussels did not receive him well. He suffered a stroke in 1866 while visiting a church, lost the power of speech, and spent the last year of his life in a nursing home in Paris, able to communicate largely through inflection and expression. He died in August 1867, aged forty-six. His mother was at his bedside.

The prose poems he had been assembling throughout his life were published posthumously as *Le Spleen de Paris*. They are the purest expression of what he had been trying to do since the inversion: the city as interior landscape, the crowd as a register of the self, the strange always constitutive of the beautiful.

He found it in the gutter. In the corpse on the roadside. In the old women watching the world from windows. In the crowds of strangers whose faces he read the way Swedenborg read the faces of angels.

The visible world is a code. Most people read the parts they find comfortable and call that sufficient.

Baudelaire read all of it, including the parts that required standing in places where comfortable people do not stand. The strangeness was the point. It was always the strangeness.
